# Tone, Voice & Sentence-Level Artifacts

## Hollow hedging

Qualifications that add nothing: "While there are certainly many valid approaches...," "The best choice depends on your specific use case and requirements...," "It's important to consider your particular context..."

**Fix:** State the tradeoff concretely. "X is faster but uses more memory" beats "the best choice depends on your specific requirements."

## False balance and diplomatic non-answers

Presenting "both sides" when one option is clearly better for the stated context.

**Pattern:** "X has its merits, but Y also has advantages. The right choice depends on your needs."

**Fix:** Have an opinion. Justify it. Acknowledge the tradeoff. Developers respect a clear recommendation backed by reasoning.

## Lack of genuine voice

AI text rarely commits to a position, uses dry humor, admits ignorance, digresses because something is interesting, or shows irregular rhythms of someone who has wrestled with the subject.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Could this have been written about any technology, or does it reflect specific experience with *this* one?
- Is there a single sentence that would surprise the reader?
- Does the writer ever say "I don't know" or "this part sucks"?
- Is there any unevenness — a section that gets more attention because the writer cares more?

## Phantom specificity

Claims that sound precise but cite nothing: "This can significantly improve performance," "Many developers find that...," "Studies have shown...," "It's widely considered best practice to..."

**Fix:** Add the number, name the source, link the benchmark, or cut the claim.

## Uniform sentence length

AI prose produces sentences of similar length and cadence. Real writing varies — short punchy statements mixed with longer, more complex constructions.

**Fix:** Read aloud. If it sounds like a metronome, rewrite for rhythm variation.

## The "This [verb]s [noun]" connector

"This ensures reliability. This improves performance. This enables teams to..."

**Fix:** Vary sentence openers. Not every sentence needs a backward-pointing demonstrative pronoun.

## Weasel phrasing

"It can be argued that...," "One might consider...," "It is generally recommended to...," "There are those who believe..."

**Fix:** Say who. If it's you, say so.

## Paired adjectives and adverbs

"quickly and efficiently," "clear and concise," "simple yet powerful"

**Fix:** Pick the one that matters. If both matter equally, find a more specific word that captures both.

## Dramatic one-liner paragraphs

Single-sentence paragraphs dropped for emphasis — a technique borrowed from LinkedIn posts and listicle content.

**Pattern:**
> "They produce completely different behavior in the failure cases that matter most."
> 
> "It wasn't."
> 
> "The cheaper model wins."

These read as performative rather than substantive. The rhetorical weight they carry is borrowed from social media conventions, which technical readers find grating. The information in a one-liner paragraph almost always belongs attached to the sentence before or after it.

**Fix:** Absorb the sentence into the surrounding paragraph. If the thought genuinely stands alone, expand it into a full paragraph that earns the emphasis through reasoning, not isolation. One-sentence paragraphs are acceptable only for transitional summary statements in long pieces (>2,500 words) where they orient the reader between dense sections — not as a stylistic tic.
